What you're actually calling about

Every company you call has years of old email, team chat, project tasks and CRM history sitting in its systems. Nobody looks at it. It costs money to store.

AI developers will now pay real money, usually six figures, to license an anonymized copy of that history. They use it to teach AI how actual work gets done inside a business.

Your job is to find out if the company you're calling is sitting on some of that money, give them an estimate on the spot, and get them a call with Logan to find out the exact number. That's it. You're not selling them anything. You're telling them about money they may not know they have.

Martal Data in one line

Say it like this

"We help companies get paid for their old business records. AI developers license a copy to learn how real work gets done. Names get stripped out, and the company decides what's included."

Who Martal Data is

Martal Data is Martal's data licensing business. We find companies sitting on years of operating records, figure out what those records are worth, and run the whole process so the company gets paid without dealing with AI developers directly.

What we do for the company

Value their records against what buyers across the market are paying, handle the anonymization, and put a training-only license in front of them with the protections written in.

Who we work with on it

Mode is our platform partner. They run the portal and the paperwork. That's why the estimate email comes from "Capital at Mode Mobile" and not from Martal.

Where we are

Brand new. The campaign launched November 10. Our first company, a 30-person software platform, signed a letter of intent after one call with Logan.

What we don't say

Don't claim customer counts, case studies or "we've done this with X." We don't have those yet. The proof on a cold call is the number they get on their own company.

Why AI developers pay for this

Today's AI tools do single tasks: draft an email, summarize a doc. The next generation is being built to run whole workflows, the way a real employee would. To learn that, AI developers need to see how real teams actually move work around.

What that looks like in a company's records

A client emails a question. Someone forwards it in Teams. A task gets opened in the project tool. The CRM gets updated. An invoice goes out. Two days later someone fixes a mistake. That chain, across tools, over years, is what they're paying for. Not the client's name. Not the dollar amounts. How the work moved.

A CRM export by itself is just a spreadsheet. The conversations around it, connected across tools, are what has value. That's why more years and more connected tools mean a bigger number.

Why a company would say yes

It's money for work already done

The records already exist. They're costing money to store and earning nothing.

It doesn't change how they run

Their team exports copies. Nothing gets connected to live systems. A few hours of someone's time.

The timing is often right

Tight margins, a partner retiring, a merger, moving off an old system, or just wanting to take some chips off the table.

Will a CEO actually listen?

Yes, if three things are true in the first 20 seconds. It's about money they already have, not something you want from them. It's specific to their company, not a blast. And it ends on a question they can answer, not a pitch they have to sit through.

What loses a CEO: sounding like a data broker ("we buy data"), a long setup, or being the 20th person who wants 30 minutes before saying anything useful. Our first signed CEO told Logan more than 20 vendors had already called him about this.

From our calls: none of the calls under 90 seconds produced a referral. Every call where the rep talked more than 45 seconds before the prospect spoke ended in a no or a hang-up. Get them talking fast.

Why these two

The value comes from records created by people working at computers: email, chat, project tools, the CRM. So the form wants how many people are creating those records, and how many years of them exist. Field crews, drivers and warehouse staff don't count, because they don't create that kind of history.

Question 1: how many people

You

"How many full-time people do you have on the team, not counting field or warehouse staff?"

For a professional firm, just ask: "How many full-time people at the firm?" Everyone there works at a computer.

"I'm not sure exactly"

"Ballpark is fine. Closer to 20 or closer to 50?"

"We have 200 people, but most are in the field"

"Got it. How many of those are in the office?" Use that number.

Question 2: how many years

You

"How long have you been on your current email and systems? And are there any older systems you moved off of but can still get into?"

"We switched last year"

"Can you still get into the old one?" If yes, that's the best case. Put the old system's years in the form too.

"Since we opened in 2012"

That's about 14 years. Put it in.

If they're not the decision maker

Before the two questions, check: "Is this something you'd decide, or is that the owner?" If it's not them, get the name first (see Objections, "Not me"). You can still ask the two questions afterward so the owner call isn't cold.

Optional, if the call is going well

  • "What runs the business day to day? Email, Teams or Slack, a project tool, a CRM?" More connected tools means more value.
  • "Anything changing this year? A merger, someone retiring, a new system?" That's your why-now for Logan.
Good to follow: fill the form while they answer. You already have the name, company and email from your list.
